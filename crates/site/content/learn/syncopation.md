A rhythm is **syncopated** when notes land between the beats and the beats themselves stay
silent or are held over. It is what makes funk, Latin music and pop grooves feel like they
push forward. Reading syncopation comes down to one skill: keep counting the beat while
you play around it.

## Offbeat eighths

The simplest syncopation: an eighth rest on the beat, the note on the "&".

```rhythm bpm=80 | Notes only on the "&"
4/4 r:e e r:e e r:e e r:e e
```

## Long notes across the beat

A quarter note that starts on the "&" lasts until the next "&" – nothing is played on
the beat in between. This is the classic "1 & – & 4" pattern.

```rhythm bpm=80 | The pop syncopation
4/4 e q q q e
```

```rhythm bpm=90 | The Charleston rhythm
4/4 q. e r:q q
```

## Sixteenth syncopations

With sixteenths, notes can fall on "e" and "a", and long notes start between them.

```rhythm bpm=70 | Sixteenth-eighth-sixteenth syncopation
4/4 s e s s e s s e s s e s
```

```rhythm bpm=70 | Funk figure with notes on "e" and "a"
4/4 e. s r:s s e r:e s s r:s e s
```

## How to read syncopation

1. **Find the beats first.** Mark where each beat starts – a long note that crosses one is
   a syncopation.
2. **Count out loud.** "1 & 2 & …" – play on the syllables that have a note.
3. **Feel the push.** A syncopated note is usually played with a little more weight.

## FAQ

### Is every offbeat note a syncopation?

Not quite. Eighth notes on "1 & 2 &" are not syncopated – the beats are still played.
Syncopation needs the beat to be silent or held over, so the accent moves to the offbeat.
